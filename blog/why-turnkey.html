Delivery Models

Why turnkey wins for industrial campuses

Mr. Sharanbasava Raj — GM, Projects 6 min read

Walk any large industrial site mid-construction and you'll often find the same pattern: a civil contractor, a PEB subcontractor, an MEPF group, a façade specialist, an interiors team and a landscape vendor — all reporting to a Project Management Consultant who reports to the client. Six accountability chains. Six progress meetings. Six invoices. Six places where blame gets passed when something slips.

This is the multi-contractor model that most owners default to. It's the cheapest model on paper. It's the most expensive model in practice.

The coordination tax

What every multi-contractor project pays — and most owners never measure — is the coordination tax. Every interface between contractors is a place where work waits. Civil finishes the slab. PEB shows up two weeks later because their material was delayed. MEPF can't start until PEB is up. Interiors can't start until MEPF is past first fix. The schedule isn't the sum of each contractor's duration — it's the sum plus every gap between handoffs.

On our largest delivery — a ₹120 Cr, 3 lakh sft food-grade facility at Kempegowda International Airport — we estimated this tax at roughly 18-22% of the project schedule. That's three months on a 14-month delivery. Three months of fixed costs, three months of client cash locked up, three months of lost revenue from a facility that isn't operational.

What a single GC changes

When one General Contractor owns the entire scope, three things change:

  • One baseline schedule. Every trade is sequenced inside one MS Project plan, not negotiated between vendors
  • One accountability chain. When MEPF rough-in is late, no one's pointing at PEB
  • One commercial closeout. No drawn-out final-bill negotiations across six vendors
"The first project we ran turnkey was for a client who'd just delivered a ₹40 Cr campus on the multi-contractor model. Their next ₹50 Cr project — same scope, same scale — came in two months faster on turnkey. They never went back."

When turnkey doesn't make sense

It's not always the right answer. Turnkey adds a margin on subcontracted scopes that you'd avoid with direct contracting. For projects where:

  • The client has a strong PMC in-house who's done this before
  • The scopes are genuinely independent (a road job + a separate landscape job)
  • Schedule isn't critical — the build can wait for the cheapest bid on each scope

... multi-contractor wins. We've turned down turnkey enquiries when the client's PMC was strong enough that they'd extract better economics doing it themselves.

The honest test

If you're scoping a project right now, ask three questions:

  • How much does one month of schedule actually cost you in lost revenue, fixed costs, and finance?
  • Do you have a PMC who can hold six contractors accountable to one plan?
  • If something goes wrong at the PEB-to-MEPF handoff, who pays?

If month-on-schedule costs are large, your PMC is thin, or you don't have a clear answer to the third question — turnkey is almost always the better economics. It's not the cheaper model. It's the model that delivers what you actually asked for.
